The platform clock jumped from 21:44 to 21:45 with a metallic click you could hear through your boots, and the air smelled faintly of wet brake dust and roasted chestnuts.

Martin checked his ticket twice, though there was nothing left to confirm. Track 7, second carriage from the rear, a seat facing backward that nobody had claimed since Thalwil. He had been carrying the same canvas bag for six years, its brass clasp polished bright by his thumb, and inside it sat an unread paperback and a wool scarf his sister had left in his hallway two winters ago.

Across the aisle, an older woman was peeling a clementine with astonishing patience, laying the rind in a single unbroken spiral on a folded newspaper.

"Is this the express," she asked, without looking up, "or the one that stops at every milk stand?"

"Stops everywhere after Sargans," Martin said.

She nodded as if this pleased her. "Good. I'm in no hurry to arrive in the dark."

***

Beyond the lake, mist had settled low against the reeds, erasing the boundary between water and sky until the streetlights along the shore seemed to float untethered. Now and then a small station slid past—empty benches, a yellow postbox, a bicycle chained to a wet railing—each one illuminated for four seconds before returning to black.

Martin leaned his forehead lightly against the cool glass. For most of the autumn he had been trying to finish a letter that required neither apology nor explanation, only an honest accounting of where the year had gone. Every draft sounded either rehearsed or defensive, as if he were arguing a case before an empty room.

He opened his notebook instead and wrote three lines:

> *Some distances aren't measured in kilometers. They are measured in the number of small Tuesdays you let pass without picking up the phone.*

The woman across the aisle offered him half the clementine. He took two segments, surprised by how sharp and bright the citrus tasted in the stale carriage air.

"You're traveling light for November," she observed.

"Just the weekend," he said. "Helping my uncle clear out his workshop."

"A dangerous business," she said gently. "You go looking for spanners and come home with forty years of someone else's weather."

***

At Landquart, two skiers boarded in heavy boots, bringing the cold scent of pine and snowmelt into the car. They sat three rows ahead, speaking in low, unhurried voices that rose and fell with the rhythm of the rails.

By the time the train curved into the valley approach, the fog had thinned enough to reveal lights scattered high on the mountainside like stray embers. Martin folded his notebook back into his coat pocket. He still didn't know how the letter would begin, only that he would write it at his uncle's kitchen table before the house woke up—in pencil, on whatever paper happened to be nearby, without crossing anything out.

When the brakes hissed to a stop under the canopy at Chur, the woman gathered her newspaper and stood.

"Tell your uncle," she said at the carriage door, "not to throw away the broken clocks. They're the only honest things in a workshop."

Martin stepped onto the cold stone platform and watched her coat disappear toward the underpass, leaving the air clear and sharp enough to wake anyone.
